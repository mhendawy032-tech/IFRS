1Recognition criteria

PP&E are tangible assets held for use in the production or supply of goods, for rental to others, or for administrative purposes, and expected to be used for more than one year.

An item is recognised as an asset when:
  • The entity has control of it.
  • Its cost can be measured reliably.
  • It is probable that future economic benefits will flow to the entity.

Examples: land, buildings, machines and equipment, furniture and fixtures, motor vehicles, computers, tools, and projects under construction.

2Initial measurement

Measured at cost on the recognition date: all costs necessary to make the asset ready for its intended use (purchased or self-constructed).

✔ Components of cost

  • Purchase price, less trade discounts and rebates
  • Import duties and non-refundable purchase taxes
  • Directly attributable costs: site preparation, initial delivery and handling, installation and testing, professional fees (architects, engineers)
  • Dismantling, removal and site restoration (IAS 37)

✘ Not part of cost

  • Administration and general overheads
  • Start-up and pre-production costs
  • Initial operating losses
  • Training
  • Waste material during construction
  • Labour downtime

Key points

  • Deferred payment: interest at a market rate is recognised or imputed (IAS 23 Borrowing Costs).
  • Self-constructed assets: same principles as acquired assets; if the entity makes such assets for sale, cost = production cost. Unusual costs (waste, downtime) are excluded.
  • Incidental income and expenses of construction (e.g. parking rent on land during site preparation) go to P/L.
  • Non-monetary exchange: measured at fair value unless (1) the exchange lacks commercial substance, or (2) the fair value of neither asset is reliably measurable.
Amendment issued 14 May 2020 (effective for periods beginning on or after 1 January 2022): proceeds from selling items produced while bringing an asset to the condition needed to operate as intended are not deducted from cost; they are recognised in profit or loss.

Dismantling (IAS 37 + IAS 16)

If a legal or constructive obligation exists and is due after more than one year, account for its present value and include it in the asset's cost:

Discount factor = 1 / (1 + interest rate)^n

3Subsequent measurement

IAS 16 permits two models:

1) Cost model

Cost − Acc. depreciation − Acc. impairment = Carrying amount (NBV)

2) Revaluation model (IFRS only)

Fair value − Acc. depreciation − Acc. impairment = Carrying amount

Only if fair value can be measured reliably.

Example – cost model

On 1/1/2020 ABC bought an asset costing $100,000 with a 5-year useful life (straight line): 100,000 ÷ 5 = $20,000 per year.

4Revaluation model – the rules

  • Carry out revaluations regularly so the carrying amount does not differ materially from fair value at the reporting date.
  • If an item is revalued, revalue the entire class of assets (class by class).
  • No fixed frequency: land and buildings may be annual; plant and equipment every 3 to 5 years.
  • Revalued assets are depreciated over the remaining useful life, as under the cost model.

Accounting for adjustments

Increase ↑

Credited to OCI and accumulated in equity as revaluation surplus, unless it reverses a previous decrease on the same asset that was expensed, in which case it is recognised in P/L up to that amount.

Decrease ↓

First reduces the revaluation surplus of the same asset; any excess is an expense in P/L.

On disposal: the revaluation surplus may be transferred directly to retained earnings (no recycling) or left in equity. It must not pass through profit or loss.

Determining fair value

  • Land and buildings: market value, usually from professionally qualified valuers.
  • Plant and equipment: market value; where unavailable (specialised nature), use depreciated replacement cost.

5Depreciation

  • The method reflects the pattern in which future economic benefits are consumed.
  • Depreciation starts when the asset is available for use (not when actually used) and continues until derecognition.
  • A systematic allocation of the depreciable amount over the useful life; charged to P/L or included in the cost of another asset.
  • Residual value, useful life and method are reviewed annually at the reporting date.
  • Changes are changes in estimates, applied prospectively (IAS 8).

6Impairment (IAS 36)

An item must not be carried at more than its recoverable amount, the higher of:

  • fair value less costs to sell, and
  • value in use (present value of future cash flows).

Impairment loss: an expense in P/L, unless it relates to a revalued asset, in which case it reduces the revaluation surplus up to the surplus held for that same asset.

7Important recognition considerations

Component accounting

Significant parts of a complex asset are depreciated separately over their own useful lives.

Replacement costs

Costs of replacing components are capitalised when recognition criteria are met.

Major inspections

The cost of each regular major inspection is capitalised in the carrying amount as a replacement.

Spare parts, stand-by and servicing equipment

  • Classified as PP&E if they meet the definition (strategic spare parts, e.g. an engine); depreciated from when ready for use, even if not used.
  • Classified as inventory (IAS 2) if they do not meet the definition (consumables).

8Derecognition

Remove the asset from the statement of financial position on disposal, or when withdrawn from use with no future economic benefits expected, or when control is lost.

Gain / Loss = Net disposal proceeds − Carrying amount → recognised in P/L

1IAS 23 – Borrowing costs: definitions

  • Borrowing costs are interest and other costs incurred by an entity in connection with the borrowing of funds.
  • A qualifying asset is an asset that necessarily takes a substantial period of time to get ready for its intended use or sale.

Borrowing costs may include

  • Interest on bank overdrafts and short-term and long-term borrowings (including intercompany borrowings).
  • Finance charges in respect of finance leases (IFRS 16 Leases).
  • Exchange differences on foreign currency borrowings, to the extent they are regarded as an adjustment to interest costs.

A qualifying asset could be

StandardQualifying asset
IAS 16 PP&EProperty, plant and equipment during the construction period
IAS 40 Investment PropertyInvestment property during the construction period
IAS 38 Intangible AssetsIntangible assets during the development period
IAS 2 InventoriesShips or planes (substantial period to complete); made-to-order inventories

2IAS 23 – Scope and accounting treatment

Two types of assets that would otherwise be qualifying assets are EXCLUDED from IAS 23:
  • Qualifying assets measured at fair value, such as biological assets under IAS 41 Agriculture.
  • Inventories manufactured or otherwise produced in large quantities on a repetitive basis that take a substantial period to get ready for sale (e.g. maturing whisky).
  • Borrowing costs directly attributable to the acquisition, construction or production of a qualifying asset form part of its cost and are capitalised.
  • All other borrowing costs are recognised as an expense.

Types of borrowing

Specific borrowings

  • Actual costs incurred
  • Less income on temporary investment of unused funds

General borrowings

  • Use a capitalisation rate (weighted average of borrowing costs)
  • Applied to expenditure on the asset
Limit: borrowing costs capitalised during a period cannot exceed the borrowing costs incurred in that period.

5IAS 23 – Commencement, suspension and cessation

Capitalisation commences when

  • Expenditures are incurred
  • Borrowing costs are incurred
  • Activities are in progress

  • Substantially all activities necessary to prepare the qualifying asset are complete

1Provision – definition and key principle

A provision is a liability of uncertain timing or amount. A liability is a present obligation arising from a past event, settled by a transfer of economic resources.

Present obligation
Past event
Transfer of an economic resource
The key principle: a provision is recognised only when there is a liability, i.e. a present obligation resulting from past events (uncertain timing or uncertain amount).

Probable means “more likely than not” (50% or more).

3Recognition criteria

An entity must recognise a provision if, and only if (remember ROT):

R – Reliable estimate

The amount can be estimated reliably (measured).

O – Obligation (present)

There is a present obligation (legal or constructive) from a past event.

T – Transfer of resources

A payment / outflow of resources is probable.

  • If the amount cannot be measured reliably, only disclose (contingent liability).
  • If settlement occurs after more than 12 months, consider the time value of money (see Measurement).

Types of obligation

1) Legal obligation

  • Contractual
  • Arises from legislation
  • Other operation of law

2) Constructive obligation

Arises if past practice creates a valid expectation on the part of a third party (e.g. a retailer with a long-standing policy of accepting returns).

Example – Smack Co guarantee

During 20X0 Smack Co guaranteed borrowings of Pony Co, whose financial condition was then sound. During 20X1 Pony Co deteriorated and on 30 June 20X1 filed for protection from its creditors.

DateTreatment
31 Dec 20X0Present obligation exists (the guarantee is the obligating event, a legal obligation), but a transfer of resources is not probable. No provision; disclose a contingent liability unless remote.
31 Dec 20X1Present obligation exists and a transfer is now probable. Recognise a provision at the best estimate of the obligation.

4Measurement

The provision is the best estimate of the expenditure required to settle the present obligation at the end of the reporting period.

One-off events

Restructuring, environmental clean-up, settlement of a lawsuit: measured at the most likely amount.

Large population of events

Warranties, customer refunds: measured at the probability-weighted expected value.

Time value of money

Where the effect is material (settlement after more than one year), the provision is the present value of the expected expenditure. The discount rate is a pre-tax rate reflecting current market assessments of the time value of money and the risks specific to the liability. The unwinding of the discount is a finance cost.

What is the debit entry?

  • The debit for a provision is not always an expense.
  • Sometimes it forms part of the cost of an asset (e.g. dismantling and restoration under IAS 16), for example when the asset is a qualifying asset that takes a substantial period to complete.

5Contingent liabilities

Applies when the likelihood is between 5% and 50%. A contingent liability is either:

1) Possible obligation

Arises from past events and its existence will be confirmed only by the occurrence or non-occurrence of uncertain future events not wholly within the entity's control.

2) Present obligation

But payment is not probable, or the amount cannot be measured reliably.

Contingent liabilities are not recognised in the statement of financial position; they are disclosed (unless remote).
If payment is probable but the amount cannot be measured, treat it as a contingent liability and disclose only.

6Contingent assets

  • A possible asset arising from past events, whose existence will be confirmed only by uncertain future events not wholly within the entity's control.
  • Contingent assets are not recognised, but are disclosed where an inflow of economic benefits is probable.
  • When realisation of income is virtually certain, the related asset is not a contingent asset and its recognition is appropriate.

7Restructuring and onerous contracts

Restructuring

A restructuring provision is recognised only when the entity has:

1) Detailed formal plan

Identifying the business / locations affected and the number of employees to be compensated for termination.

2) Valid expectation

Raised in those affected, by starting to implement the plan or announcing its main features.

3) Direct expenditures only

Only direct expenditures arising from the restructuring are provided for.

Onerous contracts

A contract in which the unavoidable costs of meeting the obligations exceed the economic benefits expected to be received. The provision is measured at the lower of:

  • the cost of fulfilling the contract, and
  • the costs / penalties of cancelling the contract.
Fulfil = $40m | Cancel = $70m → provide the lower: $40m

8Measurement and re-measurement rules

Expected disposal gains

Gains from the expected disposal of assets are not taken into account in measuring the provision.

Reimbursements from 3rd parties

Recognised only when virtually certain. Treated as a separate asset, which cannot exceed the amount of the provision.

Future operating losses

Provisions are not recognised for future operating losses.

Use and review

  • A provision is used only for the purpose for which it was originally recognised.
  • Provisions are reviewed at each reporting date and adjusted to the current best estimate.
  • If an outflow is no longer probable, the provision is reversed.

1Objective and definition

An entity may own land or a building as an investment rather than for use in the business. Such property generates cash flows largely independently of the other assets the entity holds, and is covered by IAS 40.

Investment property is property (land, a building, part of a building, or both) held by the owner, or by a lessee as a right-of-use asset, to earn rentals or for capital appreciation, or both, rather than for:
  • (A) use in the production or supply of goods or services, or for administrative purposes, or
  • (B) sale in the ordinary course of business.

2Scope – what is and is not investment property

✔ Investment property (IAS 40)

  • Land held for long-term capital appreciation
  • Land held for a currently undetermined future use
  • Building leased out under an operating lease
  • Vacant building held to be leased out under an operating lease
  • Property being constructed or developed for future use as investment property

✘ Not investment property

  • Held for use in production, supply of goods or services, or administration (IAS 16)
  • Held for sale in the ordinary course of business, or being built or developed for such sale (IAS 2)
  • Being constructed or developed on behalf of third parties (IFRS 15)
  • Leased to another entity under a finance lease (IFRS 16)
Earn rentals / capital appreciation / both → IAS 40
Production, supply or administration → IAS 16
Sale in ordinary course → IAS 2

3Recognition criteria

Investment property is a tangible, non-current asset (a resource controlled by the entity, from a past event, expected to be used for more than one period). It is recognised as an asset when:

  • Its cost can be measured reliably.
  • It is expected to be used for more than one period.
  • It is probable that the future economic benefits associated with it will flow to the entity.

4Initial measurement

Measured at cost (all costs necessary to make the asset ready for its intended use), including transaction costs.

Cost does not include: start-up costs, abnormal waste, and initial operating losses incurred before the property achieves the planned level of occupancy.

5Subsequent measurement

IAS 40 permits two models; one model is used.

1) Cost model (IAS 16)

Cost − Acc. depreciation − Acc. impairment = Carrying amount

2) Fair value model

Carrying amount = Fair value at each reporting date
  • Gain or loss on remeasurement goes to P/L
  • Investment property is NOT depreciated

Fair value: the price that would be received to sell an asset (or paid to transfer a liability) in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date.

Rules of the fair value model

  • The chosen policy is applied to ALL of the entity's investment property (one method).
  • Gains and losses from changes in fair value are recognised in profit or loss.
  • A change from the fair value model to the cost model is highly unlikely; a change is permitted only if it results in a more appropriate presentation.

Borrowing costs

  • IAS 23 (effective from 1 January 2009) mandates capitalisation of borrowing costs; this also applies to investment property under construction.
  • Entities are not required to capitalise interest on assets measured at fair value, but it is not prohibited, so this also applies to investment property under construction that is measured at fair value through P/L.

6Important classification issues

Partial own use

If part is owner-used and part earns rentals or appreciation, and the portions can be sold or leased out separately, account for them separately.

Property held under a lease

If the definition of investment property is met, it is classified as such and the lessee uses the fair value model.

Additional services

Investment property if the services are relatively insignificant (security, maintenance); owner-occupied if significant (owner-managed hotel).

Intercompany rentals

Investment property in the lessor's separate financial statements; owner-occupied in the group's consolidated statements.

Example: Rich Co owns land and the directors have not decided whether to build a factory or keep it for sale when its value rises. Because the entity has not determined that it will be owner-occupied or sold in the ordinary course of business, the land is treated as held for capital appreciation: investment property.

7Transfers to / from investment property

Transfers are made only when there is a change in use, evidenced by one or more of:

Evidence of change in useTransfer
Commencement of owner-occupationInvestment property → owner-occupied
Commencement of development with a view to saleInvestment property → inventories
End of owner-occupationOwner-occupied → investment property
Commencement of an operating lease to another partyInventories → investment property
End of construction or developmentProperty under construction → investment property

Rules (investment property at fair value)

  1. Investment property → owner-occupied or inventories: the fair value at the change of use is the “cost” under the new classification.
  2. Owner-occupied → investment property: apply IAS 16 up to the date of reclassification; any difference between carrying amount and fair value is treated as a revaluation under IAS 16.
  3. Inventories → investment property: any difference between fair value at transfer and the previous carrying amount is recognised in profit or loss.
  4. Completion of construction / development of an investment property to be carried at fair value: any difference between fair value at transfer and the previous carrying amount is recognised in profit or loss.

8Derecognition and disclosure

Derecognise on disposal, or when the property is permanently withdrawn from use and no future economic benefits are expected from its disposal.

Gain / Loss = Sales proceeds − Carrying amount → recognised in P/L

Disclosures

  • Rental income from investment property, and direct operating expenses arising from property that generated rental income during the period.
  • Direct operating expenses arising from property that did not generate rental income during the period.
  • The methods and significant assumptions applied in determining fair value.
  • Existence and amounts of restrictions on title, and investment property pledged as security for liabilities.
  • If fair value is based on a valuation by a qualified independent valuer, that fact must be disclosed.

Dec 2016 – Query two (consistency of accounting policies)

  • PP&E is accounted for under IAS 16, class by class, using the cost or revaluation model, applied consistently within a class. Property and plant are separate classes.
  • Under the revaluation model, surpluses go to OCI (unless reversing a deficit previously in P/L) and deficits go to P/L (unless reversing a surplus previously in OCI).
  • Assets with a finite life are depreciated. For property, only the buildings element is depreciable (land has an indefinite life), and buildings have a much longer life than plant, so the depreciation charge as a percentage of carrying amount is much lower.
  • Property held for investment is accounted for under IAS 40, not IAS 16. Under the fair value model it is revalued annually with changes in profit or loss and no depreciation.

1Objective

IAS 21 prescribes:

Conversion

How to include foreign currency transactions and foreign operations in the financial statements of an entity.

Translation

How to translate financial statements into a presentation currency.

The principal issues are which exchange rate(s) to use and how to report the effects of changes in exchange rates in the financial statements.

2Key definitions

TermMeaning
Functional currencyThe currency of the primary economic environment in which the entity operates. Accounting records must be kept in the functional currency.
Presentation currencyThe currency in which the financial statements are presented. There is no need to present in the functional currency.
Exchange differenceThe difference from translating a given number of units of one currency into another currency at different exchange rates.
Foreign operationA subsidiary, associate, joint venture or branch whose activities are based in a country or currency other than that of the reporting entity.

Functional currency

No choice – determined by the factors below (sales prices, input prices, labour costs).

Presentation currency

Choice – the entity decides.

3Determining the functional currency

Primary factors (priority)

  • The currency that mainly influences sales prices for goods and services (often the currency in which prices are denominated and settled)
  • The currency of the country whose competitive forces and regulations mainly determine sales prices
  • The currency that mainly influences input costs (labour, materials and other costs)

Secondary factors (supporting evidence)

The primary indicators may be determinative; these support them. The currency in which:

  • funds from financing activities are generated
  • receipts from operating activities are retained

5Foreign currency transactions

Initial recognition

Record at the spot rate at the date of the transaction. An average rate may be used if it is a reasonable approximation of the actual rates.

Monetary vs. non-monetary

Monetary items

Right to receive, or obligation to deliver, a fixed or determinable number of units of currency. Affected by changes in exchange rates.

Examples: receivables, payables, cash. Retranslated at the closing rate at every reporting date.

Non-monetary items

Absence of such a right or obligation. Not affected by changes in exchange rates.

Examples: PP&E, investment property, inventory, accrued revenue. Not retranslated at the reporting date.

Non-monetary items at fair value (PP&E and investment property revalued at the reporting date): use the exchange rate at the date of revaluation. The gain or loss follows the measurement policy: investment property → P/L; PP&E → OCI.

6Exchange differences

Monetary items

Exchange differences on settlement or on translation at the reporting date are reported in profit or loss for the period.

Revalued non-monetary items

When the gain or loss on a non-monetary item is recognised in OCI, the exchange component of that gain or loss is also recognised in OCI.

Impairment test

Non-monetary assets are carried at the lower of: carrying amount × historical rate, and recoverable amount × closing rate.

7Translation to the presentation currency

1. Establish the functional currency
2. Parent with subsidiaries → consolidate (IFRS 10)
3. Is the entity a foreign operation?
4. Translate the financial statements
  • The functional currency is the currency of the primary economic environment in which the entity operates.
  • Consolidated financial statements are prepared in accordance with IFRS 10.
  • A foreign operation (subsidiary, branch, associate or JV) has a functional currency different from the parent's.
  • Financial statements are translated to the presentation currency using the translation method.
Translation method (IAS 21 rules):
  • Assets and liabilities: closing rate.
  • Income and expenses: rates at the dates of the transactions (an average rate is normally a reasonable approximation).
  • Resulting exchange differences: recognised in OCI (equity, translation reserve).

1Definition

An asset is a resource that is controlled, measurable and expected to give future economic benefits. Tangible assets fall under IAS 16; intangible assets under IAS 38.

An intangible asset is an identifiable, non-monetary asset without physical substance, expected to be used for more than one period.

Examples

  • Computer software (non-operating systems)
  • Patented technology, including databases and trade secrets
  • Copyrights, including video and audio-visual material
  • Customer / supplier relationships, including customer lists
  • Licences, including royalty agreements
  • Import quotas
  • Franchises (a licence giving access to a franchisor's knowledge, processes and trademarks)
  • Marketing rights (exclusive rights to market the product)
Control: employee skills arising from training are unlikely to be recognised because the entity does not control the future actions of its employees. Likewise, market share and customer loyalty normally cannot be intangible assets, since the entity cannot control customers' actions.

2Recognition criteria

An intangible asset, whether purchased or self-created (at cost), is recognised if, and only if:

  • It is probable that the future economic benefits attributable to the asset will flow to the entity.
  • The cost of the asset can be measured reliably.

This applies to assets acquired externally and generated internally; IAS 38 adds extra criteria for internally generated assets (see Initial measurement).

If the criteria are not met

If an item does not meet both the definition and the recognition criteria, the expenditure is recognised as an expense when incurred. In particular, these are always expensed:

Internally generated goodwill
Start-up, pre-opening and pre-operating costs
Training costs
Advertising and promotional costs
Relocation costs

5Initial measurement

Intangible assets are initially measured at cost: all costs necessary to bring the asset to the location and condition for its intended use, including borrowing costs (IAS 23).

4. Business combinationCost = fair value at acquisition date. “Probable” is always met if fair value can be determined. Recognised separately from goodwill, whether or not the acquiree had recognised it before.
5. Internal generationResearch: expense. Development: capitalise if all criteria are met (below).

Research and development

  • Research phase: expense as incurred.
  • Development phase: capitalise if all criteria are met – remember MAIGT:
  • M – Measurement of the cost
  • A – Ability to complete
  • I – Intention to complete
  • G – Generate cash flows
  • T – Technical feasibility available
No reinstatement: an expenditure originally charged to expense cannot later be reinstated as an intangible asset.

6Subsequent measurement

Classification by useful life

1) Finite life

A limited period of benefit. Amortise over the useful life or contract period.

2) Indefinite life

No foreseeable limit to the period of net cash inflows. No amortisation; test for impairment annually.

Finite-life assets – choose either model

1) Cost model

Cost − Acc. amortisation − Acc. impairment

2) Revaluation model

Revalued amount (fair value) less subsequent amortisation and impairment losses, only if fair value is determined by reference to an active market. Active markets are uncommon for intangibles.

Possible examples: production quotas, fishing licences, taxi licences.

7Amortisation (cost and revaluation models)

  • Cost less residual value of a finite-life asset is amortised on a systematic basis over its useful life (matching principle).
  • The method reflects the pattern of benefits; if the pattern cannot be determined reliably, use the straight-line method.
  • Residual value is assumed zero unless an active market exists or a third party has committed to buy the asset.
  • The amortisation period and method are reviewed at least annually.
  • The charge is recognised in P/L (or in the cost of another asset).
  • Expected future reductions in selling prices may indicate a higher rate of consumption of the asset's future economic benefits.

8Revaluation rules and indefinite-life assets

Revaluation model – rules

  • Fair value at the revaluation date
  • Fair value determined by reference to an active market
  • If there is no active market, use the cost model
  • Revaluation is done regularly

Indefinite useful life

  • No foreseeable limit to future economic benefits
  • Not amortised
  • Impairment test annually or when an indication exists
  • Review annually whether events still support an indefinite life
  • If no longer indefinite, change to a finite useful life

9Derecognition

Derecognise on disposal, or when the asset is permanently withdrawn from use and no future economic benefits are expected from its disposal.

Gain / Loss = Sales proceeds − Carrying amount → recognised in P/L

Dec 2016 – Query three (research vs. development)

The company spent October 2015 to March 2016 researching the feasibility of a new product (charged to P/L). From April 2016 it was confident of commercial success and committed to financing development, and it developed the product during the rest of the year; development costs were capitalised.

  • Generally, internal expenditure on intangible items cannot be recognised as an asset.
  • The exception: once a development project is likely to be technically feasible, commercially viable, overall profitable and adequately resourced, future expenditure can be recognised as an intangible asset.
  • This explains the different treatment of expenditure up to 31 March 2016 (research: expense) and after that date (development: capitalise).

2018 – Query two (acquired brands vs. internally developed brand)

  • Intangible assets of acquired subsidiaries arise from a business combination; initial recognition is governed by IFRS 3: the purchase consideration is allocated to the identifiable assets and liabilities of the subsidiary.
  • A brand name is identifiable if it is separable (can be sold without selling the whole business) or arises from contractual or other legal rights.
  • Identifiable intangibles of an acquired subsidiary can be recognised separately in the consolidated statements provided their fair value can be reliably estimated.
  • The Omega brand is internally developed, and IAS 38 does not allow internally developed brands to be recognised because of the inherent difficulties in identifying and measuring them.

1Objective, scope and key definitions

IAS 10 prescribes:

  • When an entity should adjust its financial statements for events after the reporting period.
  • The disclosures about the date the financial statements were authorised for issue, and about events after the reporting period.
Beginning of reporting period
End of reporting period
Date financial statements are authorised for issue
Event after the reporting period: an event, favourable or unfavourable, that occurs between (1) the end of the reporting period and (2) the date the financial statements are authorised for issue.

2Adjusting vs. non-adjusting events

✔ Adjusting event

Provides additional evidence of conditions that existed at the end of the reporting period (including an event indicating that the going concern assumption is not appropriate for all or part of the entity).

Treatment: adjust the financial statements.

✘ Non-adjusting event

Indicative of a condition that arose after the end of the reporting period.

Treatment: do not change the recognised amounts; disclose if material.

3Examples

Adjusting events

  • Settlement of a court case after the period that confirms a present obligation at the end of the period
  • Information received indicating an asset was impaired at the end of the period
  • Customer bankruptcy after the reporting date confirming a loss on trade receivables existed at the reporting date
  • Sales of inventory after the reporting date giving evidence about net realisable value at the reporting date
  • Determination after the reporting date of the cost of assets purchased, or proceeds from assets sold, before the reporting date
  • Discovery of fraud or errors showing the financial statements are incorrect
  • Events indicating the going concern assumption is not appropriate for the whole or part of the entity

Non-adjusting events

  • Major business combinations, purchase of assets, or classification of assets as held for sale (IFRS 5)
  • Destruction of a major production plant by fire
  • A change in tax rates, or enactment or announcement of tax laws
  • Announcing a plan to discontinue an operation, or starting to implement a major restructuring
  • Entering into major commitments such as guarantees, or commencing major litigation arising from events after the reporting period
  • An abnormally large change in asset prices or foreign exchange rates, or a decline in market value of investments
  • A major ordinary share transaction or potential ordinary share transactions

4Going concern

An entity shall NOT prepare its financial statements on a going concern basis if management determines after the reporting date either that:
  • it intends to liquidate the entity or cease trading, or
  • it has no realistic alternative but to do so.
  • Prepare on a break-up basis (e.g. non-current items reclassified as current).

Disclosures required under IAS 1 if:

  • The financial statements are not prepared on a going concern basis, or
  • Management is aware of material uncertainties related to events or conditions that may cast significant doubt on the entity's ability to continue as a going concern.

5Disclosures

Authorisation of the financial statements

  • The date the financial statements were authorised for issue
  • Who gave that authorisation
  • If owners or others have the power to amend the statements after issue

Dividends

Dividends declared after the reporting period but before the statements are authorised:

  • NOT recognised as a liability at the end of the reporting period
  • Disclosed in the notes (IAS 1)

Other disclosures

  • Information received about conditions that existed at the reporting date: update the related disclosures.
  • For each material category of non-adjusting event, disclose the nature of the event and an estimate of its financial effect, or a statement that such an estimate cannot be made.

6Decision flow

QuestionAnswer → Result
1. Did the event occur between the end of the reporting period and the date the statements were authorised for issue?No → outside the scope of IAS 10. Yes → go to 2.
2. Does the event relate to a condition that existed at the end of the reporting period?Yes → adjusting event: adjust the financial statements. No → go to 3.
3. Does the event mean management will have to liquidate the entity or cease trading?Yes → prepare on a break-up basis. No → non-adjusting event: do not change recognised amounts; disclosure should generally be provided.

1Government grant vs. government assistance

Government grant

Assistance by the government in the form of transfers of resources to an entity in return for past or future compliance with certain conditions relating to the operating activities of the entity.

Government assistance

Action by the government designed to provide an economic benefit to an entity or range of entities qualifying under certain criteria. There is no transfer of resources (e.g. training, consultation).

  • Government assistance takes many forms, varying in nature and in the conditions usually attached.
  • It does not include benefits provided indirectly through action affecting general trading conditions, such as provision of infrastructure in development areas.

A government grant is recognised only when there is reasonable assurance that:

  • The entity will comply with the conditions attached to the grant, and
  • The grant will be received.
  • The grant is recognised as income over the period necessary to match it with the related costs it is intended to compensate, on a systematic basis.
  • Receipt of a grant does not prove that the attached conditions have been or will be fulfilled.
  • Non-monetary grants (land or other resources) are usually accounted for at fair value, although recording both the asset and the grant at a nominal amount is also permitted.

1Objective and scope

Ensure that assets are carried at no more than their recoverable amount, and define how recoverable amount is determined. Impairment = comparing the carrying amount with the recoverable amount (the higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use).

IAS 36 applies to (among others)

  • IAS 16: land, buildings, machinery and equipment at cost
  • IAS 16 & IAS 38: assets at revalued amounts
  • IAS 40: investment property at cost
  • IAS 38 & IFRS 3: intangible assets and goodwill
  • IAS 27, IAS 28, IFRS 11: investments in subsidiaries, associates and JVs at cost

IAS 36 does NOT apply to

  • IAS 2 – Inventories
  • IFRS 15 – Assets from construction contracts
  • IAS 12 – Deferred tax assets
  • IFRS 9 – Financial assets
  • IAS 40 – Investment property at fair value
  • IAS 41 – Agricultural assets at fair value
  • IFRS 4 – Insurance contract assets
  • IFRS 5 – Non-current assets held for sale

2Key definitions

TermMeaning
Impairment lossThe amount by which the carrying amount of an asset or CGU exceeds its recoverable amount.
Carrying amountAmount recognised in the SOFP after deducting accumulated depreciation and accumulated impairment losses.
Recoverable amountThe higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use.
Fair valuePrice received to sell an asset (or paid to transfer a liability) in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date.
Value in usePresent value of the future cash flows expected to be derived from an asset or cash-generating unit.

3When to test for impairment

At the end of each reporting period, assess whether there is any indication that an asset may be impaired. If there is, calculate the recoverable amount.

Test every year even without indicators:
  • (A) An intangible asset with an indefinite useful life
  • (B) Goodwill acquired in a business combination
  • (C) An intangible asset not yet available for use (e.g. R&D)

External indicators

  • Market value declines
  • Negative changes in technology, markets, economy or laws
  • Increase in market interest rates
  • Net assets of the company higher than market capitalisation

Internal indicators

  • Obsolescence or physical damage
  • Worse economic performance than expected
  • Asset is idle, part of a restructuring or held for disposal
  • For investments in subsidiaries, JVs or associates: carrying amount is higher than the carrying amount of the investee's assets

4Determining recoverable amount

Recoverable amount = Higher of (Fair value − costs of disposal) and (Value in use)
  • Carrying amount = cost − accumulated depreciation/amortisation − accumulated impairment.
  • Carrying amount lower than recoverable amount → no impairment. Carrying amount higher → impairment.
  • If either amount is above the carrying amount → no impairment (no need to calculate the other).
  • If fair value less costs of disposal cannot be determined → use value in use.

Fair value less costs of disposal

  • Amount obtainable in an arm's length transaction, less costs of disposal
  • Evidence: a binding sale agreement or a market price in an active market
  • Costs of disposal: incremental costs of disposing of the asset

Value in use

  • Discounted future net pre-tax cash flows from continuing use and disposal
  • Based on the asset in its current form; exclude financing activities and tax
  • Discount rate is pre-tax; risks are reflected either in the cash flows or in the discount rate

5Recognition of an impairment loss

  • Recognise a loss whenever recoverable amount is below carrying amount.
  • The loss is an expense in profit or loss, unless the asset is revalued – then it is treated as a revaluation decrease.
  • Adjust depreciation for future periods.

6Cash-generating units and allocating the loss

Cash-generating unit (CGU): the smallest identifiable group of assets that generates cash inflows largely independent of the cash inflows from other assets or groups of assets.

Goodwill

  • Goodwill must be allocated to each CGU (or group of CGUs) expected to benefit from the synergies of the combination.
  • Each unit to which goodwill is allocated must: represent the lowest level at which goodwill is monitored internally, and be not larger than an operating segment (IFRS 8).
  • Test at least annually: compare the carrying amount of the unit (including goodwill) with its recoverable amount. RA > CA → no impairment; CA > RA → recognise a loss.

Order of allocating the loss within a CGU

1. Any asset obviously damaged or destroyed
2. Goodwill allocated to the CGU
3. All other assets pro rata to carrying amounts
Never reduce an asset below the highest of: (1) its fair value less costs of disposal, (2) its value in use, (3) zero.

1The five-step model

When should revenue be recognised? Apply the five-step model:

1. Identify the contract with the customer
2. Identify the performance obligations
3. Determine the transaction price
4. Allocate the price to the performance obligations
5. Recognise revenue when (or as) each obligation is satisfied

2Step 1 – Identify the contract

A contract can be written, oral or implied. It exists when:

  • It has commercial substance
  • Rights and payment terms can be identified
  • It is agreed by each party
  • It is probable that the entity will collect the consideration
If each party has a unilateral enforceable right to terminate a wholly unperformed contract without compensating the other party, no contract exists under IFRS 15.

3Step 2 – Identify the performance obligations

A performance obligation is a promise in a contract with a customer to transfer a good or service. A contract may have one obligation or several. Each distinct obligation must be identified. A promise is distinct when:

  • It is separable from the other promises in the contract, and
  • The customer can benefit from the good or service.

Warranties

Can the customer buy the warranty separately?
If no: does it provide an additional service?
Yes to either → service-type; No → assurance-type

Assurance-type (mandatory)

  • Promises that the product is as specified and works as specified, according to the law
  • Not a separate performance obligation
  • Provide for the repair cost under IAS 37
  • E.g. a mobile phone or a car sold with a warranty

Service-type (optional)

  • Provides something additional to the mere assurance (extra services)
  • A separate performance obligation, accounted for under IFRS 15
  • Revenue is deferred and recognised over the warranty period

9Step 4 – Allocate the transaction price

The transaction price is allocated to each performance obligation on the basis of its stand-alone selling price.

Allocating a discount

  • A discount exists where the sum of the SSPs exceeds the consideration payable
  • Allocated proportionately to all obligations, unless there is observable evidence that it relates to specific obligation(s)
  • That requires: the goods or services are regularly sold on a stand-alone basis at a discount, and the discount is substantially the same as the stand-alone discount

Allocating variable consideration

Allocated entirely to one obligation (or a distinct good or service within it) if both:

  • The terms of the variable consideration relate specifically to satisfying that obligation
  • The allocation is consistent with allocating the price based on what the entity expects to receive for it

10Step 5 – Recognise revenue

The price allocated to each performance obligation is recognised as or when the obligation is satisfied, i.e. when control passes to the customer:

Over time

Control passes to the customer over some period (e.g. the contract term, a construction project).

Over time if any one applies: the customer simultaneously receives and consumes the benefits; the entity's work creates or enhances an asset the customer controls; or the asset has no alternative use and the entity has an enforceable right to payment for work done.

At a point in time

Control is retained by the supplier until it is transferred at some moment.

The “over time” conditions are the standard's criteria; they are not listed on the slides.

1Definition and introduction

Inventory: an asset held for trading in the ordinary course of business (held for sale). It is a current asset because it is sold within the operating cycle.

Inventories include

Raw materials

Materials and supplies consumed in production.

Work in progress (WIP)

Assets in the production process for sale in the ordinary course of business.

Finished goods

Held for sale in the ordinary course of business.

Why it matters

  • Inventory valuation has a direct impact on gross profit and is usually a material item.
  • Valuation is highly subjective, so many different methods are used in practice.
  • Inventory is recognised as an asset until the related revenue is recognised (the item is sold), at which point it becomes an expense (cost of sales).
  • The accruals assumption requires costs to be matched with the related revenues.

2Measurement

Inventories = Lower of (Cost) and (Net realisable value)

Cost

All costs of purchase, costs of conversion and other costs incurred in bringing the inventories to their present location and condition.

Net realisable value (NRV)

Estimated selling price in the ordinary course of business less estimated costs of completion and estimated costs necessary to make the sale.

3Components of cost

ComponentIncludes
1. Cost of purchaseImport duties, taxes, transport and handling, net of trade discounts received
2. Cost of conversionDirect labour and fixed and variable manufacturing overheads
3. Other costsCosts incurred in bringing the inventories to their present location and condition
IAS 23: borrowing costs (interest) can be included in the cost of inventories only in limited circumstances, where the inventory is a qualifying asset.

4Cost of conversion and production overheads

Directly related costs

Costs directly related to the units of production, such as direct labour.

Production overheads

A systematic allocation of fixed and variable production overheads incurred in converting materials into finished goods.

  • Variable production overheads: indirect costs that vary directly, or nearly directly, with the volume of production (e.g. indirect materials, indirect labour).
  • Fixed production overheads: indirect costs that stay relatively constant regardless of volume (e.g. depreciation of factory buildings).

Allocation rules

PointRule
Normal capacityFixed overhead is allocated on the basis of normal capacity: the average production expected over a number of periods, allowing for planned maintenance.
Actual levelMay be used if it approximates normal capacity.
Low productionFixed overhead per unit is NOT increased because of low production or an idle plant.
Unallocated overheadsRecognised as an expense in the period incurred.
Variable overheadsAllocated to each unit on the basis of the actual use of the production facilities.

5Costs excluded from inventory

Inventory cost should NOT include

  • Abnormal waste
  • Storage costs (unless necessary in the production process)
  • Administrative overheads that do not contribute to bringing inventory to its present location and condition
  • Selling costs
  • Foreign exchange differences
  • Interest cost of deferred settlement

6Cost formulas

MethodRule
Specific identificationFor items that are not interchangeable: specific costs are attributed to individual items.
FIFO / weighted averageFor interchangeable items, IAS 2 allows FIFO or weighted average.
LIFONo longer allowed.
  • Use the same cost formula for all inventories with similar nature and use. Different formulas may be justified for groups of inventories with different characteristics.
  • Standard cost and retail methods may be used to measure cost, provided the results approximate actual cost.

7Net realisable value

NRV = Estimated selling price − estimated costs of completion − estimated costs to make the sale
  • General rule: assets should not be carried at more than the amounts expected to be realised from their sale or use.
  • NRV can fall below cost when items are damaged or obsolete, or when the costs to complete or sell have increased.

Main situations where NRV is likely to be below cost

  1. An increase in costs or a fall in selling price
  2. A physical deterioration in the condition of inventory
  3. Obsolescence of products
  4. Marketing strategies to sell products at a loss
  5. Errors in production

8Recognition as an expense

EventTreatment
1. Write-downAny write-down to NRV is an expense in the period in which the write-down occurs.
2. ReversalAny reversal is recognised in profit or loss in the period in which it occurs.
3. When soldThe carrying amount is recognised as an expense in the period in which the related revenue is recognised.

9Disclosures

Disclosures include, but are not limited to:

  1. Carrying amount of inventories pledged as security for liabilities
  2. Amount of any reversal of a write-down to NRV and the circumstances that led to it
  3. Accounting policies adopted, including the cost formula used
  4. Carrying amount by classification: merchandise, supplies, materials, work in progress and finished goods
  5. Carrying amount of inventories carried at fair value less costs to sell
  6. Amount of any write-down recognised as an expense in the period

1Objective and scope

Objective: to establish the accounting for agricultural activity: the management of the biological transformation of biological assets (living plants and animals) into agricultural produce (the harvested product of the entity's biological assets).
Agricultural activity: the management of the transformation of a biological asset for sale into agricultural produce or another biological asset.

✔ IAS 41 applies to

  • Biological assets, except bearer plants
  • Agricultural produce at the point of harvest
  • Government grants related to these biological assets

✘ IAS 41 does NOT apply to

  • Land related to agricultural activity (IAS 16 or IAS 40)
  • Intangible assets related to agricultural activity (IAS 38)
  • Government grants related to bearer plants
  • Bearer plants themselves; however, it does apply to the produce growing on bearer plants

2Key definitions

TermDefinition
Agricultural activityThe management of the transformation of a biological asset for sale into agricultural produce or another biological asset.
Biological assetA living animal or plant.
Agricultural produceThe harvested produce of the entity's biological assets.
Bearer plantA living plant that: (1) is used in the production or supply of agricultural produce, (2) is expected to bear produce for more than one period, and (3) has a remote likelihood of being sold as agricultural produce.
Biological transformationThe process of growth, degeneration, production and procreation that causes an increase (or decrease) in the value or quantity of the biological asset.
HarvestThe process of detaching produce from a biological asset, or the cessation of its life.
Costs to sellThe incremental costs directly attributable to the disposal of an asset, excluding finance costs and income taxes.

3Biological transformation – the different outcomes

Asset change

  • Growth: an increase in quantity and/or quality
  • Degeneration: a decrease in quantity and/or quality

Creation of a new asset

  • Production: producing a separable non-living product (e.g. milk from a cow)
  • Procreation: producing a separable living product (e.g. a calf)

4IAS 41 or IAS 16?

For animals, the deciding factor is whether the animal is involved in agricultural activity.

Animal NOT involved in agricultural activity

Accounted for under IAS 16 (PP&E).
Example: a guard dog.

Animal involved in agricultural activity

Accounted for under IAS 41 (Agriculture).
Example: breeding puppies for sale.

6Recognition criteria

An entity recognises a biological asset or agricultural produce ONLY when:
  • The entity controls the asset as a result of past events.
  • It is probable that future economic benefits will flow to the entity.
  • The fair value or cost of the asset can be measured reliably.

7Measurement

Biological assets & agricultural produce = Fair value − Estimated costs to sell

Biological assets

  • Measured on initial recognition and at each subsequent reporting date at fair value less estimated costs to sell.
  • Exception: where fair value cannot be reliably measured (see below).

Agricultural produce

  • Measured at fair value less estimated costs to sell at the point of harvest.
  • Harvested produce is a marketable commodity, so there is NO “measurement reliability” exception for produce.

The cost-model exception (biological assets only)

A biological asset that, at the time it is initially recognised, has no quoted market price in an active market and for which alternative fair value measurements are clearly unreliable is measured at:

Cost − Accumulated depreciation − Accumulated impairment losses
Extra disclosures when fair value cannot be measured reliably: description of the assets, an explanation of the circumstances, if possible a range within which fair value is highly likely to lie, the depreciation method, useful lives or depreciation rates, and the gross carrying amount and accumulated depreciation (beginning and ending).

8Gains, losses and costs

ItemTreatment
Gain on initial recognition of biological assets, and changes in fair value less costs to sell during the periodIncluded in profit or loss
Gain on initial recognition of agricultural produce (e.g. as a result of harvesting)Included in profit or loss for the period in which it arises

9Government grants

Applies to grants related to biological assets measured at fair value less costs to sell.

Unconditional grant

Recognised in profit or loss when the grant becomes receivable.

Conditional grant

Includes grants that require the entity not to engage in certain agricultural activity. Recognised in profit or loss ONLY when the conditions have been met.

Grants related to bearer plants are outside IAS 41 (they follow IAS 20 with the bearer plant under IAS 16).

10Current versus non-current classification

IAS 1 requires an asset to be classified as current when:

  • The entity expects to sell, consume or realise the asset in its normal operating cycle.
  • The asset is held primarily for trading purposes.
  • The entity expects to realise the asset within 12 months after the reporting period.
  • The asset is cash or a cash equivalent, unless it is restricted from being exchanged or used to settle a liability for at least 12 months after the reporting period.

Non-current

A dairy or beef herd (biological asset) is shown as a non-current asset and disclosed separately.

Current

Harvested produce (e.g. milk in cold storage) is inventory under IAS 2 and is a current asset.

1Objective

IFRS 6 is guidance for entities that recognise assets used in the exploration for and evaluation of mineral resources.

Key issues

  • The initial recognition criteria and measurement basis for these assets.
  • Measurement subsequent to recognition.
  • The test for impairment of such assets in accordance with IAS 36.
Why it exists: expenditure on exploration and evaluation is excluded from IAS 16 and IAS 38. Without a specific standard, entities would use IAS 8 and practice would diverge widely. The IASB could not develop a comprehensive standard quickly, so it issued IFRS 6 to achieve some standardisation.

2Exploration and evaluation – the stages

Exploration and evaluation (E&E) is the search for mineral resources, plus determining the technical feasibility and commercial viability of extracting them, before the decision is made to develop them.

StageTreatment
1. Before the right to explore is obtainedExpense when incurred.
2. After the right to explore is obtainedExpenditure may be capitalised. The entity chooses which expenditures to capitalise under its accounting policy (IAS 8), applied consistently.
3. ProductionOnce technical feasibility and commercial viability are demonstrable, the asset falls outside IFRS 6 (impairment-tested and reclassified). The notes refer to IAS 2 for this stage.

3Elements of cost of E&E assets

  • An entity determines an accounting policy specifying which expenditures are recognised as E&E assets.
  • It should consider the degree to which expenditures can be associated with finding specific mineral resources.

✔ May be included in initial measurement

  • Acquisition of rights to explore
  • Topographical, geological, geochemical and geophysical studies
  • Exploratory drilling
  • Trenching
  • Sampling
  • Activities evaluating the technical feasibility and commercial viability of extracting a mineral resource

Obligations – IAS 37

Obligations for removal and restoration arising from the E&E undertaken are recognised under IAS 37 Provisions, Contingent Liabilities and Contingent Assets.

4Measurement

Initial measurement

When first recognised in the statement of financial position, E&E assets are measured at cost.

After recognition

Apply either the cost model or the revaluation model, per IAS 16 (tangible) or IAS 38 (intangible).

5Classification and reclassification

E&E assets are classified as tangible or intangible according to the nature of the assets acquired, and the classification is applied consistently.

ClassExample
TangibleVehicles and drilling rigs
IntangibleDrilling rights

6Impairment

Because it is difficult to obtain the information needed to estimate future cash flows from E&E assets, IFRS 6 modifies the IAS 36 rules on when such assets must be assessed for impairment.

A detailed impairment test is required in two circumstances:
  1. When the technical feasibility and commercial viability of extracting a mineral resource become demonstrable. The asset then falls outside the scope of IFRS 6 and is reclassified.
  2. When facts and circumstances suggest the asset's carrying amount may exceed its recoverable amount.

Facts and circumstances that suggest impairment

  • The period for which the entity has the right to explore in the specific area has expired during the period or will expire soon and is not expected to be renewed.

  • Substantive expenditure on further E&E in the specific area is neither budgeted nor planned.
  • Sufficient data exists to indicate that, although development is likely to proceed, the carrying amount is unlikely to be recovered in full from successful development or by sale.

2Defined contribution plan

A post-employment plan whose benefits depend on the level of contribution. The entity pays fixed contributions into a separate entity (a third party) and has no legal or constructive obligation to pay more.

1Objective and scope

Why it matters: related parties may enter into transactions that unrelated parties would not, and transactions between related parties may not be made at the same amounts as between unrelated parties.

The standard deals with

  • Identifying related party relationships and transactions.
  • Identifying outstanding balances, including commitments, between an entity and its related parties.
  • Identifying the circumstances in which disclosure of these items is required.
  • Determining the disclosures to be made about them.

IAS 24 applies to

  • Related party relationships
  • Related party transactions
  • Outstanding balances with related parties
  • Commitments to related parties

The disclosures are made in the consolidated and separate financial statements of:

A parent
Investors with joint control of an investee
Investors with significant influence over an investee

2Key definitions

Key management personnel (KMP)

Those persons having authority and responsibility for planning, directing and controlling the activities of the entity, directly or indirectly, including all directors (executive and non-executive).

Close family member

Includes, but is not limited to:

  • Children and dependents
  • Spouse / partner
  • Children and dependents of the spouse / partner

The level of influence is assessed case by case.

Related party: a person or entity that is related to the reporting entity (directly or indirectly).

3Who are related parties?

A person, or a close member of that person's family, is related to the reporting entity if that person

  • Has control or joint control over the reporting entity;
  • Has significant influence over the reporting entity; or
  • Is a member of the key management personnel of the reporting entity or of a parent of the reporting entity.

An entity is related to the reporting entity if any of these applies

  • The entity and the reporting entity are members of the same group.
  • One entity is an associate or joint venture of the other entity.
  • Both entities are joint ventures of the same third party.
  • One entity is a joint venture of a third entity and the other entity is an associate of the third entity.
Example: Mr Smith is CEO of both A and B, and A has significant influence over B. A and B are related because B is an associate of A, not merely because they share a CEO.

4Who are NOT related parties?

  • Two entities simply because they have a director or key manager in common.
  • Two ventures that share joint control over a joint venture; also providers of finance, trade unions and public utilities.
  • Departments and agencies of a government that does not control, jointly control or significantly influence the reporting entity.
  • A single customer, supplier, franchiser, distributor or general agent with whom the entity transacts a significant volume of business.
Joint venture example: A and B each hold 50% of C (a joint venture). A and B are not related to each other merely because they share joint control of C, but C is related to each of them.

5What are related party transactions?

A transfer of resources, services or obligations between related parties, regardless of whether a price is charged.

Examples (1)

  • Finance arrangements (including loans and equity contributions)
  • Purchases or sales of property and other assets
  • Purchases or sales of goods
  • Rendering or receiving of services
  • Leases

Examples (2)

  • Transfers of research and development
  • Transfers under licence agreements
  • Provision of guarantees or collateral
  • Commitments to do something
  • Settlement of liabilities on behalf of the entity

6What to disclose

  1. The nature of the related party relationship.
  2. Information about the transactions.
  3. Information about outstanding balances, to understand the potential effect on the financial statements.
  4. Information about impairment or bad debts with related parties.

1Current tax

IAS 12 prescribes the accounting treatment for income taxes: all domestic and foreign taxes based on taxable profits.

  • Current tax for the current and prior periods is recognised as a liability and an expense.
  • An overpayment of current tax is recognised as an asset.
  • Current tax liabilities or assets are measured at the amount expected to be paid to (or recovered from) the tax authorities, using tax rates enacted or substantively enacted by the end of the reporting period.

2Tax base and temporary differences

Temporary difference = Carrying amount − Tax base

Tax base of an asset

The future tax deduction that will be available when the asset generates taxable economic benefits.

Tax base of a liability

Its carrying amount, less any future tax deduction that will be available when the liability is settled.

Taxable or deductible?

  • Taxable temporary difference: settling the asset or liability will generate taxable amounts → deferred tax liability (DTL): income tax payable in future periods.
  • Deductible temporary difference: settling it will result in an allowable tax deduction → deferred tax asset (DTA): income tax recoverable in future periods.
If carrying amount − tax base is (+)If carrying amount − tax base is (−)
AssetsTaxable → DTLDeductible → DTA
LiabilitiesDeductible → DTATaxable → DTL

3Recognition and measurement of deferred tax

IAS 12 requires deferred tax to be recognised for all temporary differences, with some exceptions.

Deferred tax liability

Recognised on all taxable temporary differences (with a very limited number of exceptions).

Deferred tax asset

Recognised to the extent that it is probable that taxable profits will be available against which the deductible temporary difference can be used.

Deferred tax = Temporary difference × tax rate expected to apply when it reverses
  • The rate is based on legislation enacted or substantively enacted by the end of the reporting period.
  • Deferred tax liabilities are always shown in non-current liabilities.
Goodwill exception: IAS 12 prohibits deferred tax on the initial recognition and subsequent impairment of goodwill, so there is no deferred tax consequence from goodwill.

4Offsetting

Deferred tax liabilities and assets can be offset in the statement of financial position when:

  • They relate to the same tax jurisdiction; and
  • The reporting entity intends to settle the relevant taxable amounts on a net basis.

5Where the movement goes – P/L or OCI

A change in a deferred tax liability or asset from one period to another normally results in an adjustment to the income tax charge in P/L.

Unless the movement relates to a transaction recognised directly in other comprehensive income (for example a revaluation gain). The related deferred tax is then recognised in OCI as well.

1Overview

IFRS 16 specifies how an IFRS reporter will recognise, measure, present and disclose leases. It was issued in January 2016 and applies to annual periods beginning on or after 1 January 2019.

For the lessee, all leases are on the balance sheet. IFRS 16 removes the distinction between operating (“off balance sheet”) and finance (“on balance sheet”) leases for lessees: one accounting model for all leases.
  • All on balance sheet: a right-of-use asset and a lease liability, except (as an option) for low-value assets and short-term leases.
  • Disclosures increased: more information about the entity's leasing activities, including tables for the types of assets leased.
  • Lessors continue to classify leases as operating or finance; their accounting is substantially unchanged from IAS 17.

2Key definitions

TermDefinition
LeaseA contract, or part of a contract, that conveys the right to use an asset (the underlying asset) for a period of time in exchange for consideration.
Lease termThe non-cancellable period for which the lessee has the right to use the asset, together with (1) periods covered by an option to extend if the lessee is reasonably certain to exercise it, and (2) periods covered by an option to terminate if the lessee is reasonably certain not to exercise it.
Finance leaseA lease that transfers substantially all the risks and rewards incidental to ownership of an asset. Title may or may not eventually be transferred.
Operating leaseA lease other than a finance lease.
Interest rate implicit in the leaseThe rate that makes the PV of [lease payments + unguaranteed residual value] equal to [fair value of the underlying asset + the lessor's initial direct costs].
Lessee's incremental borrowing rateThe rate the lessee would pay to borrow, over a similar term and with a similar security, the funds needed to obtain an asset of similar value to the right-of-use asset, in a similar economic environment.

3Identifying a lease

A contract is, or contains, a lease if it conveys:
  1. The right to control the use of an identified asset
  2. For a period of time
  3. In exchange for consideration

Control is conveyed where the customer has both:

  • The right to direct the use of the identified asset; and
  • The right to obtain substantially all the economic benefits from that use.
  • An asset is typically identified by being explicitly specified in the contract, but it can also be implicitly specified at the time it is made available for use by the customer.
  • Substitution rights: where the supplier has a substantive right of substitution throughout the period of use, the customer does not have a right to use an identified asset.
A supplier's right of substitution is substantive only if the supplier has both (1) the practical ability to substitute alternative assets throughout the period of use and (2) would economically benefit from substitution.

TermDefinition
Cash-generating unit (CGU)The smallest identifiable group of assets that generates cash inflows largely independent of the cash inflows from other assets or groups of assets.

2Classification as held for sale

A non-current asset (or disposal group) is classified as held for sale if its carrying amount will be recovered principally through a sale transaction rather than through continuing use.

All of these conditions must be met:

  1. The asset (or disposal group) is available for immediate sale in its present condition.
  2. The sale is highly probable, and expected within 12 months of classification (subject to limited exceptions).
  3. The asset is actively marketed at a price reasonable in relation to its fair value.
  4. An appropriate level of management is committed to a plan to sell.
  5. Actions required to complete the plan indicate it is unlikely to be significantly changed or withdrawn.
  6. An active programme to locate a buyer and complete the plan has been initiated.

3Special cases

Acquired with a view to disposal

A non-current asset acquired exclusively with a view to its subsequent disposal is classified as held for sale at the acquisition date only if the one-year requirement is met.

Exchanges of non-current assets

Sale transactions include exchanges of non-current assets for other non-current assets when the exchange has commercial substance (IAS 16).

Held for distribution to owners

The same criteria apply to assets (or disposal groups) held for distribution to owners.

Abandonment: non-current assets to be abandoned cannot be classified as held for sale.

4Measurement

Immediately before classification

Measured in accordance with the applicable IFRS.

After classification as held for sale

Measured at the lower of carrying amount and fair value less costs to sell.

  • Impairment: recognise an impairment loss for any write-down to fair value less costs to sell, to the extent not already recognised under other IFRSs (at classification and subsequently).
  • Allocation: an impairment loss (or later gain) on a disposal group is allocated to the non-current assets in the group within the IFRS 5 measurement scope, in the order described in IAS 36.
  • Subsequent increases in fair value cannot be recognised in profit or loss in excess of the cumulative impairment losses recognised under IFRS 5 or IAS 36.
  • No depreciation: non-current assets (or disposal groups) classified as held for sale are not depreciated.

No longer held for sale

An asset that ceases to be classified as held for sale is measured at the lower of:

  1. Its carrying amount before classification as held for sale, adjusted for any depreciation that would have been recognised; and
  2. Its recoverable amount at the date of the decision not to sell.

1Objective and scope

An entity discloses information that lets users of its financial statements evaluate the nature and financial effects of its business activities and the economic environments in which it operates.

Scope: IFRS 8 applies to the separate or individual financial statements of an entity (and to the consolidated statements of a group with a parent) whose debt or equity instruments are traded in a public market.

2The CODM: chief operating decision maker

The CODM is the individual or group responsible for strategic decision making for the entity: it allocates resources to the operating segments and assesses their performance. It may be the CEO, the COO, the senior management team or the board of directors.

Engages in business activities from which it may earn revenues and incur expenses (including intersegment).

Its operating results are reviewed regularly by the CODM.

3 · Discrete information

Discrete financial information is available for it.

1Objective and scope

IAS 33 sets the principles for determining and presenting earnings per share (EPS) so that performance can be compared between entities in the same period and between periods for the same entity.

1 · Publicly traded

Entities whose ordinary shares or potential ordinary shares are traded in a public market (stock exchange).

2 · In the process of listing

Entities in the process of issuing ordinary or potential ordinary shares in a public market.

3 · Voluntary

Any other entity that chooses to disclose EPS must also comply with IAS 33.

2Key definitions

TermMeaning

DilutionA reduction in EPS (or an increase in loss per share) from assuming that convertible instruments are converted, options or warrants are exercised, or shares are issued when specified conditions are met

3Common potential ordinary shares

  • Share options and warrants
  • Convertible securities: convertible debt (bonds) and convertible preference shares
  • Contingently issuable shares, e.g. arising in a business combination
  • Contracts that may be settled in ordinary shares or cash, e.g. a debt instrument that on maturity gives the entity the unrestricted right to settle the principal in cash or in its own shares
  • Contractual rights to purchase shares, share rights and employee share purchase plans

4Presentation requirements

  • A publicly traded entity (or one in the process of issuing) presents basic and diluted EPS on the face of the statement of comprehensive income.
  • If profit or loss is shown in a separate income statement, EPS is presented only in that separate statement.
  • Basic and diluted EPS are presented with equal prominence for all periods presented.
  • They are presented even if negative (a loss per share).
  • For a discontinued operation, basic and diluted amounts per share are disclosed on the face of the statement or in the notes.

5Basic EPS

Formula: Net profit (loss) attributable to ordinary shareholders ÷ weighted average number of ordinary shares outstanding during the period

Numerator: earnings

Net profit after tax, less preference dividends, less non-controlling interest (NCI).

Denominator: weighted average shares

Shares in issue at the start of the period, adjusted for shares bought back or issued during the period, each multiplied by a time-weighting factor.

6Diluted EPS

Formula: (Basic earnings ± adjustments for assumed conversion of potential ordinary shares) ÷ (weighted average shares + new shares from assumed conversion)

Basic EPS uses the shares currently in issue. An entity may be committed to issue more shares in future (option exercise, conversion of debt). Diluted EPS shows how basic EPS would change if these potential ordinary shares became ordinary shares, so it warns current shareholders of what may happen to EPS in the future.

1What it is and who can use it

The IASB developed the IFRS for SMEs as a single standard that can be used as an alternative to full IFRS. Full IFRS is required for entities with public accountability: public entities whose securities are publicly traded and financial institutions such as banks and insurance companies.

Test is accountability, not size

Despite its title, the standard is not available to every small or medium-sized entity. It can be used only by entities that are not publicly accountable. A small listed entity cannot use it.

Permitted, not required

An entity that is not publicly accountable has the right, but not the obligation, to use it. It is one single standard, used instead of all full IFRS Standards.

2How it simplifies full IFRS

The IFRS for SMEs omits topics that matter mainly to listed entities, such as IAS 33 EPS and IFRS 8 Operating Segments. It reduces guidance and disclosure, eliminates choices and simplifies some recognition and measurement principles.

3Chapter 1: objective of general purpose financial reporting

To provide financial information about the reporting entity that is useful to existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors in making decisions. Information is prepared on the accrual basis.

What the information shows

  • The entity's economic resources, claims against the entity, and changes in those resources and claims
  • How efficiently and effectively management has discharged its responsibilities to use the entity's economic resources (stewardship)

Users' decisions

  • Buying, selling or holding equity or debt instruments
  • Providing or settling loans and other forms of credit
  • Voting, or otherwise influencing management's actions

Users assess

  • Prospects for future net cash inflows to the entity
  • Management's stewardship of the entity's economic resources

Fundamental

  • Relevance: predictive value, confirmatory value, or both. Materiality is closely related to relevance
  • Faithful representation: complete, neutral and free from error. Neutrality is supported by the exercise of prudence

Enhancing

  • Comparability: across different entities and time periods
  • Verifiability: independent and knowledgeable observers would agree
  • Timeliness: available in time to influence decisions
  • Understandability: information is classified and presented clearly

Enhancing characteristics improve the usefulness of useful information; they cannot make non-useful information useful.

An entity that is required, or chooses, to prepare financial statements. It is not necessarily a legal entity: it may be a portion of an entity or more than one entity.

A particular form of financial reports giving information about the reporting entity's assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses.

7Dec 2021, Question 4: what is the Conceptual Framework? (8 marks)

The question asks for a brief overview of the Framework's content, purpose and place in the financial reporting process. Model answer points:

  • It is a document setting out the objectives and concepts for general purpose financial reporting, and provides the foundations for IFRS Standards, but is not a standard itself.
  • The concepts are set out in distinct chapters: for example the overall objective (useful information for existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors) and the qualitative characteristics of useful financial information. Other chapter titles are acceptable, up to 1 mark.
  • A key purpose is to assist the IASB in developing and revising IFRS Standards based on consistent concepts, so the concepts behind any standard should generally be consistent with the Framework.
  • It does not override any specific IFRS Standard. In the rare case the IASB issues a standard in conflict with the Framework, it highlights the fact and explains the reasons in the Basis for Conclusions (up to 2 marks).
  • It helps preparers develop consistent accounting policies for areas not covered by an IFRS Standard (e.g. cryptocurrency) or where there is a choice of policy, and helps all parties understand and interpret IFRS Standards.

1Definition: an exit price

Fair value is the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date. It applies when another standard requires or permits fair value measurement or disclosure (including fair value less costs to sell).

Principal market

The market with the greatest volume and level of activity.

Most advantageous market

The market that maximises the amount received (or minimises the amount paid), after transaction and transport costs.

2Hierarchy: three levels of inputs

LevelInputsExample
Level 1Observable quoted prices in active markets for identical itemsListed shares
Level 2Inputs other than Level 1 quoted prices that are observable for the asset or liabilityPrices of a similar listed entity or similar asset
Level 3Unobservable inputsIncome or cash flow approach: present value of projected future earnings at an appropriate discount rate

The fair value of a non-financial asset is based on its highest and best use for a market participant, irrespective of how the entity currently uses it.

Transaction costs vs transport costs: Transaction costs are not deducted in computing fair value, because fair value is an exit price and not an entry price. Costs of transporting the asset to the market are deducted, since location is a characteristic of the asset. When a standard asks for fair value less costs to sell (e.g. IAS 41), the selling costs are then deducted as a further step.

3Which price? The bid-ask spread

Fair value is an exit measure, so for a listed share quoted with a buy (ask) and a sell (bid) price, it is the sell (bid) price that is relevant. IFRS 13 (paras 70 and 71) says the price within the bid-ask spread that is most representative of fair value in the circumstances is used, and it does not preclude mid-market pricing as a practical expedient.

IFRS 9 – Derivatives & Hedge Accounting

DerivativesHedge accountingFair value hedgeCash flow hedge
DerivativesHedge accountingFair valueCash flowExam applicationPast exams

1Derivatives: a contract whose value follows an underlying

A derivative will be settled at a future date, requires no (or a low) initial investment, and changes in value in response to an underlying (interest rate, financial instrument price, commodity price, exchange rate or index). Types: forward contracts, futures, swaps, options.
Example: a bread maker buys wheat futures at a fixed price for six months ahead. If wheat prices rise, it gains on the contract; if they fall, it loses. All derivatives are measured at fair value, with changes in profit or loss unless hedge accounting applies.

2Hedge accounting: optional, to match gains and losses

The hedged item (a future wheat purchase, or inventory at lower of cost and NRV) may not be recognised or may be measured differently from the derivative. Hedge accounting matches the gains or losses on the hedging instrument with those on the exposure.

  • A relationship qualifies only if it consists only of eligible hedging instruments and eligible hedged items
  • At inception there is formal designation and documentation of the relationship, the risk management objective and the strategy
  • It meets all hedge effectiveness requirements
TermMeaning
Hedged itemAn asset, liability, firm commitment or forecast transaction that exposes the entity to changes in fair value or cash flows, and is designated as hedged
Hedging instrumentA designated derivative (or in limited cases another financial asset or liability) expected to offset changes in the hedged item
Hedge effectivenessThe degree to which changes in the hedged item attributable to the risk are offset by the hedging instrument

3Fair value hedge

Hedges changes in fair value of a recognised asset or liability, or an unrecognised firm commitment, for a particular risk that could affect profit or loss.

  • Remeasure the hedging instrument to fair value, with the gain or loss in profit or loss.
  • Adjust the hedged item's carrying amount for the gain or loss from the hedged risk only, also in profit or loss.
  • The two largely offset, so the net effect is only any ineffectiveness.
  • If the hedged item is an equity instrument at FVOCI, both gain and loss go to OCI.

4Cash flow hedge

Hedges variability in cash flows of a recognised asset or liability, or a highly probable forecast transaction, for a particular risk that could affect profit or loss.

  • Effective portion of the instrument's gain or loss: OCI, accumulated in a cash flow hedging reserve in equity.
  • Ineffective portion: profit or loss.
  • Recycle the amounts from OCI to profit or loss when the hedged cash flows affect profit or loss (e.g. when interest income or expense is recognised).
Basis adjustment: When a cash flow hedge of a forecast purchase or firm commitment results in a non-financial asset (e.g. PP&E), the accumulated amount in the cash flow hedge reserve is transferred directly into the asset's initial carrying amount. This transfer does not go through OCI again.

Structured summaries of recognised assets, liabilities, equity, income, expenses and cash flows.

  • Statement of financial position "at the end of the period"
  • Statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income "for the period"

Secondary financial statements

  • Statement of changes in equity "for the period"
  • Statement of cash flows "for the period"
  • Notes: summary of significant accounting policies and other explanatory information

Must be displayed prominently, and repeated as necessary

  • The name of the reporting entity and any change in the name
  • Whether the statements are for a group of entities or an individual entity
  • Information about the reporting period
  • The presentation currency (as defined by IAS 21)
  • The level of rounding used (e.g. thousands, millions)

Comparative information

  • Comparative information for the preceding period is required as part of the complete set (not required by US GAAP).
Three statements of financial position (including one at the beginning of the earliest comparative period) are required where the entity:
  • A. applies an accounting policy retrospectively, or
  • B. makes a retrospective restatement of items (error), or
  • C. reclassifies items (by error). Example: an item recognised as inventory that should be classified as PP&E.

2 · Two statements

6Investing and financing: the basic rule

Financing

Items related to obtaining finance (e.g. interest on borrowings, bond repayments).

Investing

Income and expenses from assets not used in the entity's main business (e.g. income from investment properties).

The investing category

For an entity that has a specified main business activity, classify in the investing category income and expenses from:

  • a) investments in associates, joint ventures and unconsolidated subsidiaries
  • b) cash and cash equivalents; and
  • c) other assets if they generate a return individually and largely independently of the entity's other resources

The income and expenses from these assets comprise the amounts in profit or loss for:

  • a) the income generated by the assets
  • b) income and expenses from initial and subsequent measurement, including on derecognition
  • c) incremental expenses directly attributable to acquisition and disposal (e.g. transaction costs, costs to sell)

7The financing category

To decide what goes in the financing category, distinguish between two kinds of liability:

(a) Liabilities from transactions that involve only the raising of finance

Except as set out in paragraphs 63–66, classify in financing:

  • Income and expenses from initial and subsequent measurement, including on derecognition
  • Incremental expenses directly attributable to issue and extinguishment (e.g. transaction costs)

(b) Liabilities from transactions that do not involve only the raising of finance

Classify in financing:

  • Interest income and expenses, but only if the entity identifies them for the purpose of applying other requirements in IFRS Accounting Standards
  • Income and expenses from changes in interest rates, on the same condition

Current vs. non-current

An entity normally presents a classified statement of financial position, separating current and non-current assets and liabilities, unless a presentation based on liquidity provides reliable information.

Current assets

  • Expected to be realised in the normal operating cycle
  • Held primarily for trading
  • Expected to be realised within 12 months after the reporting period
  • Cash and cash equivalents (unless restricted)

All other assets are non-current.

Current liabilities

  • Expected to be settled in the normal operating cycle
  • Held for trading
  • Due to be settled within 12 months
  • The entity has no right at the end of the period to defer settlement beyond 12 months

Other liabilities are non-current.

Example: if a liability has become payable on demand because the entity breached an undertaking under a long-term loan agreement on or before the reporting date, it is current, even if the lender agreed, after the reporting date and before the statements were authorised, not to demand payment because of the breach.

12Worked exam example

Omega – June 2022 (8 marks): two queries on the statement of profit or loss and OCI

Omega's and Rival's statements both show revenue, finance costs, profit before tax and income tax, but the classification of operating expenses differs completely. The query asks how this is possible under full IFRS, and how items are allocated between profit or loss and OCI.

QueryAnswer
1. Why can the line items differ?The overall presentation requirements are in IAS 1, which requires certain key items (e.g. revenue, income tax expense) and presentation of other line items so they are relevant to understanding performance. Operating expenses are presented by nature or function, whichever is more reliable or relevant. So the line items can be quite different while both comply with IFRS.
2. P/L or OCI: is there free choice?All income and expenses go in profit or loss unless another IFRS requires or permits otherwise. There is no theoretical distinction, but gains are more likely than losses to be in OCI (revaluation gains in OCI; revaluation losses usually in P/L). P/L items feed EPS, OCI items do not, and EPS is a key indicator for listed entities, so it matters for both Omega and Rival.

Reduced disclosuresEligibilityPublic accountabilityExceptions
ObjectiveDisclosures onlyEligibilityPublic accountabilityWorked exam examplesKit questions

1Objective

IFRS 19 specifies the disclosure requirements an entity is permitted to apply instead of the disclosure requirements in other IFRS Accounting Standards.

An entity electing to apply IFRS 19 applies the requirements in other IFRS Accounting Standards (recognition, measurement and presentation) except for the disclosure requirements. Instead, it applies the disclosure requirements in IFRS 19.
Example: reporting inventory
Recognition, measurement and presentationApply the relevant IFRS Accounting Standard: here, an eligible subsidiary applies IAS 2 Inventories.
Disclosure requirementsDo not apply the disclosure requirements in IAS 2. Instead apply the disclosure requirements in IFRS 19, under the subheading "IAS 2 Inventories".

2Disclosures only: what changes and what does not

An entity applying IFRS 19 is not required to apply the disclosure requirements in other IFRS Accounting Standards, nor any statements about, or references to, those disclosure requirements.

Exceptions: if an entity applying IFRS 19 also applies IFRS 8 Operating Segments, IFRS 17 Insurance Contracts or IAS 33 Earnings per Share, it applies all the disclosure requirements in those standards.

Still applied from other standards

  • Recognition
  • Measurement
  • Presentation

Replaced by IFRS 19

  • The disclosure requirements of other IFRS Accounting Standards
  • In accordance with IFRS 18, an entity applying IFRS 19 need not give a specific IFRS 19 disclosure if the resulting information would not be material.
  • A company applying IFRS 19 must state in the notes to the financial statements that it has done so.

3Eligibility

An entity may elect to apply IFRS 19 in its consolidated, separate or individual financial statements if, and only if, at the end of the reporting period:
  • a) it is a subsidiary;
  • b) it does not have public accountability; and
  • c) it has an ultimate or intermediate parent that produces consolidated financial statements available for public use that comply with IFRS Accounting Standards.

Eligible entities are permitted, but not required, to apply IFRS 19.

4Public accountability

An entity has public accountability if:

1 · Publicly traded instruments

Its debt or equity instruments are traded in a public market, or it is in the process of issuing such instruments for trading in a public market (a domestic or foreign stock exchange or an over-the-counter market, including local and regional markets).

2 · Fiduciary capacity

It holds assets for a broad group of outsiders as one of its primary businesses (e.g. banks, credit unions, insurance companies, securities brokers/dealers, mutual funds and investment banks often meet this criterion).

DefinitionsJoint operation vs ventureAccountingWorked exam examplesPast exams

1Joint arrangement and joint control

TermDefinition
Joint arrangementAn arrangement between two or more parties that have joint control.
Joint controlThe contractually agreed sharing of control of an arrangement: decisions about the relevant activities require the unanimous consent of the parties sharing control.
IFRS 11 specifies two types of joint arrangement: a joint operation and a joint venture.

2Joint operation vs. joint venture

The distinction depends on whether the investors have direct rights and obligations connected with the separate assets and liabilities of the arrangement, or an interest in the net assets of the arrangement.

Joint operation

The parties with joint control have rights to the assets, and obligations for the liabilities, relating to the arrangement.

  • Likely where the arrangement is merely a vehicle for the more effective operation of the investors' existing businesses
  • No investment recognised in the SOFP and no investment income in the SOPL

Joint venture

The parties with joint control have rights to the net assets of the arrangement.

  • Likely where the arrangement carries out a business basically separate from the businesses carried out directly by the individual investors
